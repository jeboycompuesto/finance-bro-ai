# Financebro — “Can I tell them yes?”

Conversation-led demo film · target 2:40 · maximum 3:00

## Creative direction

A founder gets a call from their excited cofounder about expanding into New York. As they work through the decision, the founder uses Financebro on the laptop. Each question motivates a visible product interaction. The audience follows one conversation from possibility to a better-informed next step.

Reference: [Cofounder video and breakdown](https://x.com/titouangillet_/status/2095521587785081193?s=20). The post and selected video frames were inspected in the browser, including the outdoor setting and laptop close-up; this draft also uses the user's description of the grandma conversation. It is not a transcription or a shot-for-shot recreation.

The cofounder is the recommended caller: they have a natural reason to discuss launch costs, hiring, and revenue timing. Only their voice is needed. Play both characters as thoughtful people who want the business to succeed. The caller is excited; the founder becomes more confident as the model clarifies the tradeoffs.

**One location, one visible actor, one phone voice, one laptop.** A quiet table by a window or on a shaded terrace works. Start with the laptop visible. Move between the founder's face, an over-the-shoulder view, and readable screen captures. Keep the call audible through those changes. The walkthrough occupies the middle of the scene; no separate “now let me show you the demo” segment is needed.

**Build basis:** This is a proposed narrative based on the product brief and mockups, not a verified recording of the application. Record the working flow first, use labeled fictional company data, and lock the result dialogue to the actual outputs. The primary ending below requires a result that supports a smaller launch over a full rollout.

## 0:00–0:12 · The opportunity

**Picture:** Medium shot. Founder at a table with Financebro open. Phone vibrates; founder answers on speaker. Small opening caption: “A founder's next big decision.” Keep a discreet “Demo company” label on product footage.

**Cofounder:** “They've got a space for us in New York. Next quarter. Can I tell them yes?”

**Founder:** “I want to. Give me a second.”

**Action:** Founder pulls the laptop closer. Cut on that movement into the screen.

## 0:12–0:30 · The actual question

**Picture:** Over the shoulder, then a clean screen recording of the current cash and runway. Do not attempt to make the audience read a distant, angled laptop screen.

**Cofounder:** “We have the money, right?”

**Founder:** “We have cash today. I want to know what we'll have after we open.”

**Cofounder:** “What are you looking at?”

**Founder:** “Financebro. I'm checking what the plan does to our cash.”

**Action:** Show the founder enter and submit: “Can we expand into New York next quarter?” Hold long enough to read it.

## 0:30–0:55 · Turn the conversation into inputs

**Picture:** Financebro's follow-up questions. Brief close-up of hands typing, then return to readable UI. The requested fields must match the running build.

**Founder:** “We'd need two people there?”

**Cofounder:** “Two to start. And the launch budget I sent you.”

**Founder:** “When do we expect customers to start paying?”

**Cofounder:** “Month three. That's the plan.”

**Founder:** “Okay. Let's put that in.”

**Action:** Fill the actual launch-cost, hiring, and revenue-timing inputs. Show the minimum cash reserve too if the build supports it. Prepare numerical inputs before filming; do not fill this sequence with typing.

## 0:55–1:20 · Let the product explain the plan

**Picture:** Submit the model. Briefly show genuine processing if present, then the resulting forecast. Match-cut from the physical laptop to the screen capture at the same UI state. Keep one full view long enough to establish the chart before zooming into the relevant result.

**Cofounder:** “So what does it say?”

**Founder:** “Here's our cash if we keep going as we are. And here's the expansion. The launch and the new hires go out before the extra revenue comes in.”

**Action:** Trace baseline versus expansion with a deliberate cursor movement. Hold the chart in silence for a few seconds so viewers can understand the difference.

**Result cue:** This line assumes the chart shows that relationship. Adapt to the actual modeled cost and revenue timing.

## 1:20–1:50 · The question that changes the answer

**Cofounder:** “And if the customers pay late?”

**Picture:** Cut briefly to the founder looking back at the laptop.

**Founder:** “Let's see what two months does.”

**Action:** Visibly change revenue timing and run the update. Show the old and updated forecast in sequence. Keep the relevant axis labels and amounts in frame. Give the changed result a short beat before the founder interprets it.

**Founder:** “That takes our lowest cash balance from [actual baseline amount] to [actual delayed-payment amount]. We'd be below the reserve we wanted to keep.”

**Cofounder:** “Okay. What if we start smaller?”

**Result cue:** Replace the brackets with the actual displayed values. The reserve line is conditional on the real result. If the product shows runway instead, use the displayed before-and-after runway. If timing cannot be edited but a downside scenario works, say “Let's check the downside case” and select it. Do not imply that a generic downside scenario represents precisely two months of delayed payments.

## 1:50–2:15 · Find a workable next step

**Picture:** Reduce the launch scope using a supported input or select an existing smaller-launch scenario. Show the recalculated result and its explanation. Keep the new costs visible so viewers understand what changed.

**Founder:** “Let's try one hire and a smaller launch.”

**Action:** Make the edit, submit, and let the result appear.

**Founder:** “That leaves more room if payments come late. We'd still need to watch when the revenue arrives.”

**Cofounder:** “So, can I tell them yes?”

**Founder:** “Tell them we're interested in starting smaller. Let's work out that version.”

**Result cue:** Use this version only if the modeled result supports it. If it does not, use: “Even the smaller launch leaves us tight. Let's wait until we have more cash coming in.” The call should end with a clear next step grounded in the result.

**If another recalculation is not available:** Show the existing recommendation and its drivers. Founder: “The pressure point is when the revenue arrives. Send me the smaller launch budget. I want to model that before we commit.” Do not show a fabricated second calculation.

## 2:15–2:30 · Human payoff

**Picture:** Back to the original medium shot. Founder leans back; the laptop remains in the composition.

**Cofounder:** “Glad we checked.”

**Founder:** “Me too.”

**Cofounder:** “Coffee?”

**Founder:** “That one I can say yes to.”

**Action:** A small smile. End the call. Let the moment breathe; avoid delivering the last line like a punchline in an advert.

## 2:30–2:40 · Product close

**Picture:** Final clean product shot, then Financebro wordmark.

**Voiceover:** “Financebro turns your financial data and business plans into a model you can question. Your next move, modeled.”

**End card:**

Financebro

Your next move, modeled.

## Shoot and edit notes

- Record the product flow first and use its real results to finalize the dialogue. Rehearse the full scene against that recording. These timings are an editing plan, not a measured performance.
- Show a click, typed prompt, or assumption edit before each corresponding output. The phone call provides the story; do not suggest the product hears or acts on the call unless it actually supports that workflow.
- Keep dialogue running over the screen recordings. Leave short pockets of silence for reading the question, seeing the comparison, and noticing the changed result.
- Record a clean screen capture separately from the camera footage. Use straight-on screen inserts or a matched transition from the laptop; screen replacement is optional and should use actual captured UI.
- Frame the actor and laptop together for context. Use full-screen product footage for inputs, charts, and recommendations that the audience needs to read.
- Use restrained captions and only a few emphasis labels: “The plan,” “Payments arrive late,” and “Start smaller.” These are editorial captions, not invented product UI.
- Film in shade or indirect window light to control screen reflections. Capture the remote voice separately for intelligibility and mix it to sound lightly like a phone call.
- Preserve actual result order when shortening loading time. Avoid edits that imply an unimplemented feature or a response to an input the app never received.
- Keep the film between 2:30 and 2:50 if the performances need more room. Cut repeated explanation before shortening the key forecast comparison. Check the final exported duration stays under 3:00.

## Other creative routes

**Calling Mum:** Warmer and more personal. Start with “How's the business?” and “We're thinking about New York.” Her question “And if it takes longer than you think?” triggers the downside test. Best if a real family member is comfortable on the call; write around their natural voice.

**Present you calls future you:** More playful. Future you says, “Ask what happens if they pay late.” Use the same performer in two framings. This makes scenario testing memorable, but needs more editing and a clearly hypothetical framing so the model isn't presented as predicting the future.

**Recommended:** The cofounder call. It is easy to shoot with the team, gives every product interaction a natural reason, and lets the financial decision carry the story.
